Conventional Loans — The Standard

What makes a loan 'conventional,' how Fannie and Freddie work, and what your buyers need to qualify
~10 min readDRE Chapter 12 / FNMA / FHLMC Guidelines5 Quiz Questions
📋 Setting the Scene

Marcus and Priya's lender calls back: "Good news — you both qualify for a conventional loan." Marcus asks you: "What's a conventional loan? And how is that different from FHA?" Great question — and every buyer's agent should be able to explain the basics.

What Is a Conventional Loan?

A conventional loan is any mortgage not backed by a federal government agency. It is NOT insured by FHA, guaranteed by VA, or backed by USDA. Instead, it is funded by private lenders and typically sold to Fannie Mae (FNMA) or Freddie Mac (FHLMC) — the two government-sponsored enterprises that create a secondary market for mortgages.

📌 The Secondary Market in Plain English

Your buyer's lender doesn't keep the loan forever. After closing, they typically sell it to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac. This replenishes the lender's capital so they can make new loans. Fannie and Freddie set the rules (underwriting guidelines) that lenders must follow to sell them loans — which is why conventional loan requirements are fairly standardized nationwide.

Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI)

If the buyer puts less than 20% down on a conventional loan, the lender requires Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI). PMI protects the lender — not the buyer — if the borrower defaults.

  • Typical PMI cost: 0.5% to 1.5% of the loan amount per year
  • On a $1,000,000 loan: $5,000–$15,000 per year added to carrying costs
  • PMI can be removed once the buyer reaches 20% equity (either through payments or appreciation)
  • By law (Homeowners Protection Act), lenders must automatically cancel PMI when the loan balance reaches 78% of original purchase price
✅ Real Example for Marcus and Priya

If Marcus and Priya put 10% down on $1,250,000 (loan of $1,125,000), PMI at 0.8% would cost approximately $9,000/year — $750/month added to their payment. Putting 20% down ($250,000) eliminates PMI entirely. Their lender can help them decide if the extra down payment makes financial sense for them.

Jumbo Loans

When a loan exceeds the conforming loan limit ($806,500 in most areas; up to $1,209,750 in high-cost California counties in 2025), it becomes a jumbo loan. Jumbo loans have stricter requirements:

  • Typically require 20-30% down
  • Credit score minimums are usually higher (720+)
  • Interest rates can be slightly higher or similar to conforming, depending on market
  • Cannot be sold to Fannie/Freddie — lender holds or sells on private markets

For a $1,250,000 purchase in Los Angeles, even with 20% down, the loan would be $1,000,000 — well above the conforming limit. Marcus and Priya will likely need a jumbo loan or a high-balance conforming loan depending on LA County's limit.
