The Natural Hazard Disclosure (NHD)

Flood zones, fire hazard areas, earthquake fault lines — what California requires sellers to disclose about the land itself
~10 min readCivil Code §1103 / RPA ¶10A / Q(1)5 Quiz Questions
📋 Setting the Scene

5555 Winners Lane is in Los Angeles. The Okonkwos love the neighborhood — but Priya is from the Midwest and has never thought about earthquakes or wildfires. She asks you: "Is this house in a danger zone?"

Good thing California law requires an answer to that question in writing before they close.

What Is the NHD Report?

The Natural Hazard Disclosure (NHD) report is required under Civil Code §1103 for all residential property sales in California. It discloses whether the property is located in any state-designated natural hazard zone.

Hazard ZoneWhat It Means
Special Flood Hazard Area (SFHA)FEMA-designated 100-year flood zone; may require flood insurance
Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone (VHFHSZ)State-designated high wildfire risk; specific defensible space requirements
Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI)Areas where development meets undeveloped wildland — elevated fire risk
Earthquake Fault Zone (Alquist-Priolo)Active fault zones where new structures for human occupancy are restricted
Seismic Hazard ZoneAreas with potential for liquefaction or landslides during earthquakes
State Fire Responsibility Area (SRA)CAL FIRE manages fire protection here instead of local fire department
RPA Reference: RPA ¶10A and ¶Q(1) address the NHD report. Cost is negotiable — either party can pay. A third-party NHD company typically prepares the report for $100–$200.

Who Prepares It and Who Pays?

NHD reports are typically prepared by specialized third-party companies (such as Natural Hazard Disclosure Source, Property ID, or similar). The seller is responsible for ensuring the buyer receives it, but the cost is negotiable under RPA ¶Q(1) — buyer, seller, or split.

📌 Timing

Like the TDS, the NHD must be delivered before the close of escrow. Buyer gets the same 3-day (personal delivery) or 5-day (mail) cancellation right after receiving it.

What Happens If a Property Is In a Hazard Zone?

Being in a hazard zone doesn't necessarily kill a deal — but buyers need to know what they're taking on. For example:

  • Flood zone: Lender may require flood insurance (can add $1,000–$3,000/year to carrying costs)
  • Fire hazard zone: Homeowner's insurance can be harder to obtain — and very expensive. Some properties in VHFHSZ zones face insurance non-renewals
  • Earthquake fault zone: New construction restrictions; seismic retrofit may be required
⚠️ Post-Wildfire Reality in California

After major fires in LA, Ventura, and other counties, many insurance carriers have stopped writing policies in VHFHSZ zones. As the buyer's agent, always advise clients to confirm insurance availability BEFORE removing the insurance contingency.

Local Option Disclosures

Beyond the state-mandated NHD zones, some NHD reports also include local ordinances, tax assessments (Mello-Roos), airport influence areas, and environmental hazards. These are called "local option" disclosures and are often included in the same report package.

✅ Practical Tip

When you receive the NHD for your clients, walk them through each checked box. Don't just say "here's a document" — explain what each hazard zone actually means for insurance costs, building restrictions, and daily life.
