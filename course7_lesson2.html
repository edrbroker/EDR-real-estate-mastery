Getting Pre-Approved — What It Really Means

The difference between pre-qualification and pre-approval, and why it matters when writing competitive offers
~10 min readDRE RE-22 / Reg B (ECOA) / TILA5 Quiz Questions
📋 Setting the Scene

You've asked Marcus and Priya to get pre-approved before you start touring homes. They come back three days later and say: "We got pre-qualified online — it only took 2 minutes." You take a breath and explain why that's not the same thing as a pre-approval — and why it won't get their offer accepted in this market.

Pre-Qualification vs. Pre-Approval — The Real Difference

TermWhat It MeansHow Strong Is It?
Pre-QualificationBuyer self-reports income, assets, debt. Lender estimates what they might qualify for. No verification. No credit pull.Weak — not meaningful in a competitive offer
Pre-ApprovalBuyer submits full application. Lender verifies income, pulls credit, reviews assets. Lender issues a written commitment pending property appraisal.Strong — what sellers and listing agents want to see
Fully Underwritten ApprovalUnderwriter has reviewed and approved the borrower file. Only condition remaining is the property appraisal. Strongest possible position.Strongest — rare but compelling in multiple offer situations
📌 Bottom Line for Buyers

A 2-minute online pre-qualification is essentially meaningless in a competitive California market. The listing agent will ask: "Is it pre-approved or pre-qualified?" Sellers want to see verification of income and credit — not just a computer estimate.

What the Lender Reviews for Pre-Approval

  • Credit score — most conventional loans require 620+; 740+ gets the best rates
  • Income documentation — W-2s, tax returns (2 years), recent pay stubs, or profit and loss if self-employed
  • Assets — bank statements showing funds for down payment and closing costs
  • Employment history — typically 2 years of consistent employment in the same field
  • Debt-to-income ratio (DTI) — most conventional loans require DTI under 43%; some programs allow up to 50%
DRE Reference: The DRE's RE-22 publication (Sources of Home Loans) advises buyers to obtain credit reports BEFORE contacting a broker or lender. Knowing their credit score in advance lets buyers address errors and understand where they stand before beginning the application.

The DTI Ratio — Simple Math That Matters

Debt-to-Income ratio (DTI) compares your client's monthly debt payments to their gross monthly income. Lenders use two ratios:

  • Front-end DTI (housing ratio): New monthly mortgage payment ÷ gross monthly income. Typically must be under 28–31%.
  • Back-end DTI (total debt ratio): All monthly debt payments (mortgage + car + student loans + credit cards) ÷ gross monthly income. Typically must be under 43%.

Example for Marcus and Priya: Combined gross income = $20,000/month. Estimated mortgage payment on $1,250,000 with 20% down at 7%: approximately $6,650/month. Front-end DTI = 6,650 / 20,000 = 33% — borderline for most conventional loans. Their loan officer can advise on whether they qualify and how to optimize.

✅ Help Your Buyers Prepare

Before your clients apply for pre-approval, advise them to: (1) pull their free credit reports at annualcreditreport.com to catch any errors, (2) avoid opening new credit cards or making large purchases, (3) not quit their job or switch industries, (4) gather 2 years of tax returns. These simple steps make the pre-approval process smoother and faster.
