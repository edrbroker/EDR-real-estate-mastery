Your Fiduciary Duty in Negotiations

The legal and ethical foundation of representing clients — and why negotiation starts before you write the offer
~10 min readCivil Code §2079.13 / B&P Code §101765 Quiz Questions
📋 Setting the Scene

Marcus and Priya Okonkwo are ready to make an offer on 5555 Winners Lane. As their agent, you know the listing price is $1,250,000. You also know from a conversation with the listing agent that the sellers are motivated — they need to close in 30 days.

That's valuable information. What you do with it defines your fiduciary duty.

Fiduciary Duty — What It Actually Means in Negotiations

As a buyer's agent, you owe your clients a full fiduciary duty under California Civil Code §2079.13. In a negotiation context, this means six specific obligations:

DutyWhat It Means in Negotiations
LoyaltyYour client's interests come first — always. Not the commission, not keeping peace with the other agent, not closing fast to move on.
ConfidentialityYou cannot share your client's maximum price, financial situation, or motivation with the other side without consent.
DisclosureYou must share any information that could affect your client's decision — including what you learn about seller motivation.
ObedienceYou follow lawful client instructions — even if you personally disagree with their offer strategy.
Reasonable CareYou negotiate competently, using market data and experience to inform every recommendation.
AccountingAll funds, EMD, and credits are properly tracked and documented.
📌 The Key Tension

You learned the sellers need to close in 30 days. This is information that benefits your clients — it suggests the sellers may be flexible on price or terms. Your duty to disclose means you tell the Okonkwos. Your duty of confidentiality means you don't tell the other agent what your clients' ceiling is. These duties work together, not against each other.

What You Cannot Do in Negotiations

Under B&P Code §10176, agents can lose their license for:

  • Telling the other side your client's maximum price without consent
  • Steering your client toward a higher offer to maximize your commission
  • Discouraging a client from making an offer because you personally want a different outcome
  • Making misrepresentations during negotiations (about the property, other offers, timelines)
  • Using confidential client information to benefit anyone other than your client
⚠️ Commission Pressure

Your commission is a percentage of the purchase price. Higher price = higher commission. This creates a potential conflict of interest. California's fiduciary duty standard requires you to put your client's interests first — even if a lower offer would serve them better. Recommending an unnecessarily high offer to boost your commission is a B&P §10176 violation.

Negotiation Starts Before the Offer

The best negotiators build leverage before they ever write a number on the contract. That means:

  • Market research: Know what comparable homes actually sold for — not just asking prices
  • Days on market: A listing that's been sitting 45+ days has far less leverage than a new listing
  • Seller motivation: Estate sales, divorces, job relocations — any of these signal flexibility
  • Competing buyers: Is there a multiple offer situation, or are you the only buyer?
  • Property condition: Inspection history, deferred maintenance — these affect your negotiating position
✅ Your First Call After Showing

Every time you show a property your clients like, call the listing agent and ask two questions: "How has the response been on the property?" and "Is the seller flexible on timeline?" You'll be surprised how much information you can gather by simply asking professional, friendly questions.
