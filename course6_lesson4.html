Multiple Offer Situations

How to navigate a bidding war — legally, ethically, and strategically
~10 min readRPA ¶3A-¶3F / C.A.R. Form MOPB / DRE Guidelines5 Quiz Questions
📋 Setting the Scene

The listing agent calls you at 4 PM: "We have 4 offers coming in tonight. Best and final by 5 PM tomorrow." The Okonkwos have until tomorrow to decide how hard they want to fight for 5555 Winners Lane.

Multiple offer situations are where buyers get hurt — and where great agents shine.

What Sellers Can and Cannot Do in Multiple Offers

In a multiple offer situation, sellers have significant flexibility — but agents on both sides have ethical obligations. The DRE and C.A.R. Code of Ethics govern this process.

Sellers CAN:Sellers CANNOT:
Accept any offer, for any reason, in any orderDiscriminate based on protected characteristics (Fair Housing Act)
Counter one offer while ignoring othersAccept two offers simultaneously (creates binding contract issues)
Ask for "best and final" from all buyersShare one buyer's offer terms with another buyer's agent to gain leverage
Accept a lower offer for non-price reasons (timeline, terms)Create a fake competing offer to pressure a buyer
📌 Disclosure of Multiple Offers

In California, sellers are NOT required to tell buyers how many other offers exist. The listing agent may disclose that multiple offers are present (if the seller authorizes it), but must be careful not to selectively reveal terms that unfairly advantage one buyer over another.

The "Best and Final" Request

When a seller asks for "best and final," they want each buyer to submit their strongest offer by a deadline without further back-and-forth. This is your clients' one shot. Counsel them clearly:

  • Price: What is the absolute most they can and will pay? Put that number in
  • EMD: Increase if possible — it demonstrates commitment
  • Contingencies: Shorten timelines if your client is comfortable and prepared
  • Close: Match seller's preferred timeline exactly
  • Personal letter: Controversial (Fair Housing concerns) — defer to your broker's policy
⚠️ Buyer's Letters — Fair Housing Risk

Buyer personal letters to sellers can create Fair Housing liability. If a letter contains information about race, religion, national origin, familial status, or other protected classes, it can expose the listing agent and seller to a discrimination claim. Many brokerages have banned them entirely. Always check EDR policy before recommending one.

Escalation Clauses — Use With Caution

An escalation clause says: "I'll pay $X above the highest competing offer, up to a maximum of $Y." They can be effective, but carry risks:

  • Sellers may not accept them (they reveal your ceiling)
  • They can be exploited if the seller's agent is not ethical
  • They require proof of the competing offer to be valid under most interpretations
  • Some sellers see them as a sign you don't know what you want to pay
✅ EDR Best Practice

In most situations, a clean offer at the highest price your client is willing to pay is stronger than an escalation clause. Discuss escalation clauses with your broker before using them. Never use one without fully explaining the risks to your client.
