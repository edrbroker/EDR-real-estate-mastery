The Appraisal — How Lenders Value Property

What appraisals are, how they affect your transaction, and what to do when one comes in low
~10 min readUSPAP / RPA ¶14L(2) / FIRREA5 Quiz Questions
📋 Setting the Scene

The Okonkwos are three weeks into escrow. Their lender orders the appraisal. A week later, the report comes back: the property appraised at $1,205,000 — $45,000 below their purchase price of $1,250,000. Marcus panics. "Does this mean the deal is off?" You take a deep breath and walk them through exactly what this means and what their options are.

What Is a Real Estate Appraisal?

An appraisal is a licensed appraiser's professional opinion of market value for a specific property on a specific date. Lenders require an independent appraisal before funding a loan because they will only lend based on the LOWER of the purchase price or appraised value.

📌 The Golden Rule of Appraisals

The lender bases the loan on the LOWER of the purchase price or appraised value. If your buyers offer $1,250,000 and the property appraises at $1,200,000, the lender calculates the down payment and loan based on $1,200,000 — not the purchase price. Your clients must cover the $50,000 gap in cash or renegotiate.

Who Orders the Appraisal and Who Pays?

  • The lender orders the appraisal (to ensure appraiser independence)
  • The buyer pays for it — typically $500–$900 in California
  • Paid upfront or at closing depending on the lender
  • Buyers are entitled to a copy of the appraisal report

Appraisers must be state-certified in California (regulated by the Office of Real Estate Appraisers — OREA) and must follow the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice (USPAP). An appraiser is completely independent — neither the buyer, seller, nor agent can influence their conclusion.

The Appraisal Contingency — RPA ¶14L(2)

Under RPA ¶14L(2), buyers have an appraisal contingency that gives them the right to cancel and recover their EMD if the property appraises below the purchase price. The default deadline is 17 days after acceptance.

What the Buyer Can Do When the Appraisal Is LowHow It Works
Renegotiate the priceRequest that the seller reduce the price to the appraised value — common in buyer-friendly markets
Cover the gap ("appraisal gap guarantee")Buyer brings additional cash to cover the difference between appraisal and purchase price — common in competitive markets
Split the differenceSeller reduces price partially; buyer covers the rest
Cancel under the contingencyBuyer cancels and recovers EMD — must be done before removing the appraisal contingency
Challenge the appraisal (reconsideration of value)Submit comparable sales the appraiser may have missed; lender reviews. Sometimes succeeds, often doesn't.
RPA Reference: RPA ¶14L(2) is the appraisal contingency. If the buyer removes this contingency in writing before an appraisal issue is resolved, they give up the right to cancel based on appraisal. Never advise a client to remove the appraisal contingency without fully understanding the risk.

For Marcus and Priya — A $45,000 Gap

With a $1,250,000 purchase price and a $1,205,000 appraisal, the Okonkwos have a $45,000 gap. Their options:

  1. Ask the seller to reduce price to $1,205,000
  2. Cover the $45,000 gap in cash (increase down payment)
  3. Split it — seller comes down $20,000; buyers cover $25,000
  4. Cancel under the appraisal contingency and get their EMD back
✅ Set Expectations Before Appraisal

In a competitive market, buyers sometimes offer above list price. Prep them in advance: "If you offer over asking, there's a risk the property won't appraise at that price. Here's what your options would be." Buyers who know this upfront handle low appraisals without panic.
