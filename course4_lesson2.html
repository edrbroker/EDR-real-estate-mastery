Title Insurance 101

What it is, why your buyer needs it, and what it actually protects against
~10 min readRPA ¶13 / Title Insurance5 Quiz Questions
📋 The Scenario

Escrow has opened on 5555 Winners Lane. The escrow officer calls and asks Marcus and Priya what type of title vesting they want. Priya says, "Can you just explain what title even means?" You step in and walk them through it. This is one of those moments that separates a good agent from a great one.

What Is Title?

"Title" is the legal right to own and use a property. When you buy a home, the seller transfers title to you through a legal document called a grant deed. But what if someone else has a claim on that property you didn't know about? That's where title insurance comes in.

What Does Title Insurance Protect Against?

Title insurance protects against defects in the chain of title that existed before you bought the property. Common title issues include:

  • Unpaid liens — a previous owner owed money and a creditor placed a lien on the property
  • Forged documents — a fraudulent deed was recorded somewhere in the history
  • Undisclosed heirs — a deceased prior owner's heir comes forward with a claim
  • Recording errors — mistakes in the public record that affect ownership
  • Boundary disputes — encroachments from neighboring properties
📌 Key Distinction

Unlike car or home insurance that protects against future events, title insurance protects against past events — things that happened before the current sale. You pay a one-time premium at close of escrow, and the policy lasts as long as you own the property.

Two Types of Title Insurance Policies

There are two policies in most California residential transactions:

  • Lender's Policy (ALTA) — required by the buyer's lender. Protects the lender's interest up to the loan amount. The buyer pays for this.
  • Owner's Policy (CLTA or ALTA Homeowner's) — protects the buyer's equity in the property. In most California counties, the seller customarily pays for the owner's policy, but this is negotiable in the RPA.
RPA Reference — ¶13: The RPA specifies which party pays for title insurance. In Southern California, it is customary for the seller to pay for the CLTA owner's policy. Always check local custom and what was negotiated in the offer.

The Preliminary Title Report (Prelim)

Before issuing a title insurance policy, the title company researches the property's history and issues a Preliminary Title Report (the "prelim"). The prelim shows all recorded documents affecting the property — liens, easements, CC&Rs, taxes owed. Your buyer has the right under the RPA to review and approve the prelim during the contingency period.

✓ Pro Tip

Read the prelim carefully. Look for items in Schedule B (exceptions to coverage) — these are things title insurance will NOT cover. Unpaid Mello-Roos, easements, and existing CC&Rs are common Schedule B items that directly affect your buyer.
