Common Disclosure Mistakes That Get Agents in Trouble

Real DRE violation patterns — and how to protect yourself and your clients
~10 min readBusiness & Professions Code §10176 / Civil Code §2079 / DRE Regulations5 Quiz Questions
📋 Setting the Scene

In any given year, hundreds of California real estate licensees face DRE disciplinary action. A significant portion of those cases involve disclosure failures — things that could have been avoided with better habits and knowledge.

You've built the foundation. Now let's talk about where agents get tripped up.

B&P Code §10176 — The Agent Misconduct Statute

Business & Professions Code §10176 lists the grounds on which the DRE can suspend or revoke a California real estate license. Disclosure-related violations that appear here include:

  • Making any substantial misrepresentation
  • Making any false promises
  • Pursuing a course of misrepresentation through agents
  • Acting in a dual capacity as agent and undisclosed principal
  • Commingling trust funds with personal funds
⚠️ License on the Line

A DRE complaint can result in a formal accusation, administrative hearing, and suspension or revocation of your license. Even if you "win," the process is expensive, time-consuming, and damaging to your reputation. Prevention is the only real answer.

Building a Disclosure Habit

The most protected agents in California aren't the ones who know the most laws — they're the ones who have built consistent disclosure habits into every transaction. Here's how:

  • Checklist: Use a transaction checklist that includes every required disclosure with a delivery date and signature confirmation
  • Timing: Deliver TDS, SPQ, NHD, and AVID as soon as possible after acceptance — never at the last minute
  • Client education: Walk buyers through every disclosure form. Don't just hand them paper
  • Documentation: Keep copies of every signed disclosure in your transaction file
  • Broker: When in doubt, call your broker. That's what they're there for
✅ The Simple Rule

If you're asking yourself "do I need to disclose this?" — the answer is almost always yes. The cost of over-disclosure is zero. The cost of under-disclosure can be your license.
