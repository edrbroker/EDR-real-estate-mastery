Fixed vs. Adjustable Rate Mortgages

How interest rates work, what ARMs are, and why your clients need to understand the difference before they sign
~10 min readDRE Chapter 12 / TILA Reg Z5 Quiz Questions
📋 Setting the Scene

The Okonkwos' lender gives them two options: a 30-year fixed at 7.125% or a 5/1 ARM at 5.875%. The ARM sounds great — $750/month less. But Marcus calls you: "Which one should we pick?" You know you can't answer that — but you need to explain what each one actually means so they can make an informed decision with their lender.

Fixed-Rate Mortgages

A fixed-rate mortgage has the same interest rate for the entire life of the loan — 15, 20, or 30 years. The monthly principal and interest payment never changes.

  • Predictability: Your clients always know what the payment will be
  • Higher initial rate: Because the rate is locked for 30 years, lenders charge more than adjustable rates
  • Best for: Buyers who plan to stay long-term, or who want certainty in their budget
📌 Most Common Choice in California

The 30-year fixed is the most common home loan in California and the U.S. It is the baseline most buyers use to compare other loan types. Per the DRE's Chapter 12 on Real Estate Finance, the fixed-rate conventional loan was "the typical financing vehicle" for residential purchases in stable economic environments.

Adjustable-Rate Mortgages (ARMs)

An ARM has an interest rate that is fixed for an initial period, then adjusts periodically based on a market index. Common ARM structures include:

ARM TypeFixed PeriodThen AdjustsBest For
5/1 ARM5 years fixedAnnually after Year 5Buyers planning to sell or refinance within 5 years
7/1 ARM7 years fixedAnnually after Year 7Buyers planning to stay 5-7 years
10/1 ARM10 years fixedAnnually after Year 10Buyers planning to stay 7-10 years

How ARM Adjustments Work

After the fixed period ends, the rate adjusts based on a market index (commonly SOFR, replacing the old LIBOR) plus a margin set by the lender. ARMs have caps that limit how much the rate can increase:

  • Initial cap: Maximum increase at first adjustment (often 2%)
  • Periodic cap: Maximum increase at each subsequent adjustment (often 2%)
  • Lifetime cap: Maximum increase over the life of the loan (often 5-6%)
DRE Warning (Chapter 12): "Because ARM interest rates can increase over the term of the loan, ARM borrowers share with lenders the risk that interest rates will increase; therefore, it is important for borrowers to not only fully understand how their loan may react to changes in market conditions, but also to minimize their exposure by selecting loan programs with low margins and reasonable caps."

Interest-Only Loans

An interest-only loan requires the borrower to pay only interest for a set period (typically 5-10 years), with no principal reduction. After that period, the payment jumps significantly to pay off the full principal in the remaining term. These are available primarily for jumbo loan borrowers and are high risk for buyers who don't plan carefully.

⚠️ Payment Shock Warning

The biggest risk with ARMs and interest-only loans is "payment shock" — the sudden, large increase in monthly payment when the rate adjusts or the interest-only period ends. The DRE's Chapter 12 specifically identifies payment shock as a major risk associated with alternative mortgage products. Always make sure your clients understand this risk before they choose an ARM.

✅ Your Role

You can explain what fixed-rate and adjustable-rate loans are in plain English. What you cannot do is tell clients which is right for them. After explaining the concepts, always say: "Let's get you on a call with your lender so they can run the numbers based on your specific situation."
