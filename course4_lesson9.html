HOA Transactions — What Escrow Requires

The extra documents, fees, and timelines when the property has a homeowners association
~10 min readRPA ¶11 / Civil Code §45255 Quiz Questions
📋 The HOA Curveball

Imagine 5555 Winners Lane is in an HOA community. The seller's agent calls and says the HOA package won't be ready for 10 days. Your client asks: "Can't we just skip it?" The answer is no — and here's why.

What the Seller Must Provide

Under California Civil Code §4525 and the RPA ¶11, if the property is in a Common Interest Development (CID) — which includes condos, townhomes, planned unit developments, and many single-family HOA communities — the seller must provide a package of HOA documents to the buyer. This includes:

  • CC&Rs (Covenants, Conditions & Restrictions)
  • HOA Bylaws and Articles of Incorporation
  • Current Rules and Regulations
  • Most recent 12 months of HOA meeting minutes
  • Most recent financial statements and budget
  • Reserve study
  • Any pending special assessments or litigation involving the HOA
RPA ¶11 / Civil Code §4525: The seller must deliver HOA documents to the buyer. The buyer then has 3 days (if delivered in person) or 5 days (if mailed) to cancel based solely on the HOA documents — this is a separate cancellation right, not tied to the standard contingency period.

HOA Fees at Escrow

HOA transactions add fees to the closing statement:

  • Transfer fee — charged by the HOA when ownership changes (often $100–$500)
  • Document preparation fee — for compiling the HOA package
  • Move-in/move-out fees — some HOAs charge these separately
  • Proration of dues — monthly HOA dues are prorated at close between buyer and seller
📌 Reserve Fund Health

One of the most important things to review in the HOA package is the reserve fund. A well-funded HOA has 70%+ of its recommended reserve. An underfunded HOA is a red flag — it often means a special assessment (a large one-time bill to all owners) is coming.

Special Assessments — The Hidden Cost

A special assessment is a charge levied against all HOA homeowners for a major expense (roof replacement, pool repairs, structural issues). If the HOA hasn't saved enough in reserves, they can pass a special assessment — sometimes in the tens of thousands of dollars per unit. Always check the meeting minutes and financials for any mention of upcoming assessments.

⚠️ Red Flag Question to Ask

Always ask the listing agent: "Is there any pending or anticipated special assessment?" And then read the HOA minutes yourself — because what an agent "heard" and what's actually in the minutes can be very different.
