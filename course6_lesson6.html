Seller Credits and As-Is Offers

When and how to use seller credits strategically — and what as-is really means under California law
~10 min readRPA ¶3Q / ¶14C / Civil Code §11025 Quiz Questions
📋 Setting the Scene

The Okonkwos are in escrow on 5555 Winners Lane. After negotiations, the seller agreed to give them a $12,000 credit toward closing costs instead of making repairs. Meanwhile, you're starting to prep for a future buyer who wants to make an as-is offer on a different property.

Two different situations — and both require you to understand exactly what the law allows.

How Seller Credits Work

A seller credit (also called a seller concession) is an amount the seller agrees to contribute toward the buyer's allowable closing costs. It is applied at closing and appears on the Closing Disclosure (CD) as a credit to the buyer.

Loan TypeMaximum Seller Credit
Conventional (less than 10% down)3% of purchase price
Conventional (10–24% down)6% of purchase price
Conventional (25%+ down)9% of purchase price
FHA6% of purchase price
VA4% of purchase price
All CashNo lender restrictions
📌 The $12,000 Credit for the Okonkwos

The Okonkwos are putting 10% down on a conventional loan. The purchase price is $1,250,000. Maximum allowable seller credit = 6% = $75,000. Their $12,000 credit is well within limits — and will apply toward their closing costs at settlement.

What "As-Is" Actually Means in California

When a buyer makes an "as-is" offer, they are indicating they intend to purchase the property in its current condition without asking for repairs. But "as-is" does NOT mean:

  • The seller is relieved of disclosure obligations — the TDS, SPQ, NHD, and AVID are still required
  • The buyer waives the right to inspect — inspections are still allowed
  • The buyer cannot cancel based on inspection results — they can still cancel within the contingency period
  • The seller can hide known defects — concealment is still fraud
Legal Note: Civil Code §1102 makes seller disclosure mandatory regardless of "as-is" language. An as-is clause does not override California disclosure law. It only means the buyer is not asking for repairs upfront — it does not waive their right to discover facts and cancel.
⚠️ As-Is Misunderstanding

Some sellers and agents believe an as-is designation protects against any post-closing claims. It does not. Sellers who conceal known defects can still be sued for fraud — as-is or not. The disclosure obligations of the TDS, SPQ, and Civil Code §2079 apply regardless of contract language.

When to Use a Seller Credit vs. a Price Reduction

ToolBest UseKey Consideration
Seller CreditBuyer has limited cash for closing costs; or to fund repairs post-closeSubject to lender limits; cannot exceed allowable closing costs
Price ReductionProperty has significant issues affecting appraised valueAffects loan-to-value ratio; requires new appraisal if substantial
Repair CompletionHealth and safety issues; items requiring licensed contractorsQuality and timing control is with seller; harder to manage
✅ When Credits Beat Repairs

Credits are almost always faster and cleaner than repairs. Sellers don't have to manage contractors, buyers get cash at closing to apply however they choose, and the deal stays on track. Whenever possible, negotiate a credit rather than a repair list.
