The Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS)

California's most important disclosure form — what it covers, who fills it out, and what happens if it's wrong
~10 min readCivil Code §1102 / RPA ¶14A / N(1)5 Quiz Questions
📋 Setting the Scene

The Okonkwos just got into contract on 5555 Winners Lane. Day 7 after acceptance arrives and their agent receives a stack of documents from the listing side — including the Transfer Disclosure Statement.

Priya flips through it and asks: "Do we have to read all of this?" The answer is: yes, every line. And as their agent, you need to understand it better than they do.

What Is the TDS?

The Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) is a California-mandated disclosure form required under Civil Code §1102 for the sale of residential property (1–4 units). It requires the seller to disclose known material facts about the property's condition.

Think of it like a report card for the house — but the seller fills it out themselves. Your job as the buyer's agent is to review it carefully and flag anything that looks concerning.

📌 Who Is Required to Fill Out the TDS?

The seller and the seller's agent both have separate sections of the TDS. The seller discloses what they know about the property. The seller's agent discloses what they observed during their visual inspection. You, as the buyer's agent, also conduct a visual inspection and complete your portion.

What Does the TDS Cover?

  • Appliances and systems (roof, plumbing, electrical, HVAC)
  • Known defects or repairs made during ownership
  • Neighborhood nuisances (noise, odors, flooding)
  • Legal issues (lawsuits, code violations, easements)
  • HOA information and special assessments
  • Room additions or permits
RPA Reference: RPA ¶14A requires seller to provide the TDS within 7 days of acceptance (or as otherwise agreed). Buyer has a right to cancel within 3 days of receipt (or 5 if mailed) under Civil Code §1102.3.

The Three Sections of the TDS

SectionWho Fills It OutWhat It Covers
Section I — Coordination of InspectionsAgent (coordinates)Scheduling and inspection logistics
Section II — Seller's InformationSeller (all owners)Known defects, repairs, systems, legal issues
Section III — Agent's Visual InspectionBoth agentsWhat each agent observed walking the property
⚠️ Common Agent Mistake

Completing Section III with "no known defects" without actually walking the property is a Civil Code §2079 violation. Your visual inspection must be genuine. A DRE audit or lawsuit can expose this quickly.

What Makes a Defect "Material"?

A material fact is anything that would affect a reasonable buyer's decision to purchase the property or the price they're willing to pay. If there's any doubt about whether something needs to be disclosed — it does.

✅ Agent Tip

Coach your sellers to "over-disclose." It's always better to list a repaired roof leak than to hide it. Undisclosed defects are the #1 source of post-closing lawsuits in California real estate.

TDS and the Okonkwos

When reviewing the TDS for 5555 Winners Lane, you notice the seller checked "yes" to a prior roof repair but left the explanation blank. You immediately follow up with the listing agent for documentation — when was it repaired, by whom, and is there a permit? This protects your clients from an undisclosed defect claim later.
